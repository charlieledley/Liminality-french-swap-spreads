Ruptures in US bond markets have, understandably, been taking up quite a lot of headspace lately.
Continued bond market woes are important, and the US Treasury market is the most important of
them all. Unless you’re French.
Because it won’t have escaped any French bond-type’s attention that the market for OATs is having
a moment. And by moment we mean a bad one.

But this is to compare apples and pears.
US Treasury prices are sinking/yields are rising for some combination of reasons (a higher
expected path for Fed funds, rising term premia, higher breakeven inflation expectations), and
while some of the rise in French bond yields is going to reflect Eurozone versions of these, these
should be captured also in changes in German bond yields.
The government has said the deficit will widen to 5.5 per cent of GDP this
year, well above target. Slowing economic growth — France now lags its
neighbours — has also complicated efforts to improve its finances.

Never in the post-Whatever-It-Takes- era have 10-year and 30-year French bond spreads traded as
high as they do now over their German equivalents.
Are investors spooked by polls showing that the Eurosceptic far-right Marine Le Pen is on track to
win the presidency next year? Or by leftist challenger Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s idea to cancel some of
France’s debt? Or maybe just by the seeming inability of the current minority government to win
any kind of support from left or right to shrink next year’s budget deficit down to a mere 5 per cent
of GDP?
