As hundreds of thousands of people poured on to the streets of France to protest against planned
budget cuts last week, the bond market made clear its own deep discontent with the country’s
public finances.
Teachers, nurses and civil servants marched against what they denounce as unbearable austerity in
a demonstration of the public anger that could sink the government’s plans to rein in its deficit. In
parallel, the brutal sell-off in the country’s bonds took borrowing costs close to 5 per cent, their
highest in almost a quarter of a century.
“Schools with no money, a country with no future,” read one sign held aloft by a protester in Paris.
Angry students have adopted more radical tactics by shutting down high schools across France,
clashing with police and setting destructive fires, leading to 5,000 arrests.
France is caught between the protesters on the street calling for more funds from the government
and “bond vigilantes” in the markets who punish perceived fiscal irresponsibility by betting against
the country’s debt.
Not only does it now cost France more to borrow than it does Italy and Greece; a rising number of
big French companies enjoy lower market interest rates than does the French state. Of all the G7
nations, it has suffered most from the debt sell-off triggered by the Iran war.

The fear among investors is that the country could be tipped into a full-blown debt crisis that could
not only rock France’s economy but also shake the Eurozone.
On Monday, the euro fell to a 17-month low against the dollar as worries intensified about the
impact of the French debt sell-off on the single-currency area.

In the worst-case scenario, some think the European Central Bank could be forced to come to the

“France has been sleepwalking down the path that leads towards a financial crisis,” says Mike

The budget showdown
In Paris, ministers and former officials play down the idea that France could fall prey to a full￾blown debt crisis. They cite the country’s solid economic and institutional foundations — high
domestic savings, reliable tax collection and a stable banking sector.
As the global bond market sells off, France is not alone in facing rising long-term yields, which
move inversely to prices, nor have the country’s fundamentals worsened overnight, they say. Bond
auctions continue to be oversubscribed.
“France is not Greece during the Eurozone crisis,” says Moulin, the Bank of France chief. “If it can
pass a budget this year to reduce spending and narrow the deficit as the government has proposed,
then markets will be reassured by this concrete step of fiscal consolidation.”

In any case, investors are sceptical that the budget can deliver spending cuts to reach Lecornu’s
goal of bringing the fiscal deficit down to 5 per cent in 2027. This year, instead of holding steady as
hoped, the deficit is projected to rise to 5.4 per cent because of the economic slowdown and higher
borrowing costs. France once promised to rein in the deficit back within the EU limits of 3 per cent
of GDP by 2029; at present such targets are firmly out of reach.
Next year’s presidential race provides still more uncertainty.
Marine Le Pen, the far-right politician leading the polls, sought on Tuesday to reassure the markets
and voters by promising €140bn in budget cuts over five years.
Amid the bond rout, she has called for a national debt limit of 60 per cent of GDP — half of current
levels — to drag the country back “from the brink of a financial, economic and moral crisis”.
But she is also campaigning on lowering the retirement age for some and slashing VAT on energy
and food essentials — which could add tens of billions to the debt.

And yet the fiscal plight of the Eurozone’s second-largest economy is worsening. France has not
balanced its budget since 1974; even when interest payments are excluded, it has run a deficit every
year since 2002.
Increasing taxes to deal with the rising cost of debt servicing could also be perilous. At 44 per cent
of GDP, the tax burden is already among the world’s highest; the average in the OECD club of rich
countries is 34 per cent.
Almost a quarter of public spending is gobbled up by pensions. French retirees leave the workforce
earlier and receive relatively generous benefits by OECD standards. Attempts to push back the
retirement age trigger angry protests.
Eric Lombard, who served as finance minister last year, says there is no way to fix public finances
without increasing the retirement age and reducing unemployment. “The fundamental problem in
France is we don’t work enough,” he says. But if more people are in employment and benefits
curtailed, he adds, with “a bit of luck in five years we can turn things around”.

A vicious spiral
Nowhere has France’s declining economic status been more apparent than in the bond market.
Paris was long deemed a “semi-core” Eurozone borrower for its safe status, trading at a modest
spread to German Bunds. Debt trouble spots such as Italy and Greece, by contrast, were badged as
the Eurozone “periphery” for their greater debt risks and higher spreads.
France’s political gridlock and worsening debt have made things very different. A series of ratings
downgrades has helped push up the country’s borrowing costs, which passed Greece’s in late 2024
and Italy’s last year. Investors now label France the “new periphery”.
Despite the government’s tax-raising powers, its borrowing costs now exceed those of big French
multinationals such as LVMH, the luxury group, and Sanofi, the pharmaceutical company.
According to Goldman Sachs, more than a quarter of investment-grade French companies had a
lower yield on their bonds than comparable government debt as of the end of September.
All big western economies have endured rises in borrowing costs since the end of the pandemic.
Benchmark interest rates have returned to more normal levels after Covid-era lows. Central banks
scrapped their vast asset-buying programmes just as bond issuance from sovereign and corporate
borrowers hit record levels, while Japanese investors have also pulled back from French bonds and
other Eurozone debt.

The phenomenon has gained pace since the outbreak of the Iran war, as central banks have raised

But France has been hit hardest among big bond markets, as investors demand a much bigger

Downgrading French government debt last month, the rating agency Scope said rising yields would
hurt the country through “further constraining fiscal flexibility and increasing the scale of the
adjustment required to stabilise public debt”. A further test will come in a review by Moody’s late
this month.
Market interest rates take time to feed through into countries’ debt servicing costs, but France’s are
already painfully high. The government expects interest payments to total €79bn this year and
€91bn, or 2.9 per cent of GDP, in 2027.

Even those figures are based on a yield below the current market rate; they assume an average of

The European dimension
The widening spreads between French bonds and those of the country’s European neighbours have
set off market speculation about the ECB stepping in.
French government officials and some opposition figures privately signal that such outside
intervention may be the only way to force deep spending cuts.
Alain Minc, an influential adviser to French corporations who has informal ties to the government,
argues that the crisis could snowball even before the presidential election.
“The question that investors will be asking themselves is if the ECB is prepared to support France
with no strings attached until the presidential election,” he says. “The mere fact that people are
asking will make the crisis worse.”
Bond investors are also debating whether a certain level of French spreads above German Bunds
would force the ECB to step in to calm the markets that determine Eurozone economic conditions.
Most think the central bank would be especially likely to act if there is serious contagion from
France to other European debt markets.
“The move is no longer just about France,” wrote TD Securities strategist Pooja Kumra in a note
last week, as European bonds sold off, together with other areas of the debt market. “Broader
financial tightening may require a more forceful response from the ECB to help stabilise markets.”
A much bigger sell-off may be needed for the ECB to act, but investors are aware that political
conditions in France could amplify the risk.

Two years later, the central bank announced it had designed an emergency bond-buying tool to
fight “unwarranted” and “disorderly” swings in bond prices that are a “serious threat to the
transmission of monetary policy”.
Details of this so-called Transmission Protection Instrument are opaque, and it has never been
used so far. Analysts think that the hurdles are high; in the summer of 2022, the ECB declined to
intervene when the Italian spread with Germany shot up to 2.5 points, more than a percentage
point above the French spread’s current level.
The ECB said it would only use the instrument to buy the bonds of countries that pursue “sound
and sustainable fiscal and macroeconomic policies”, an argument that might be difficult to make
for France. “Widening spreads without clear fiscal consolidation efforts will not trigger TPI,” says
Carsten Brzeski at ING.
Morgan Stanley’s chief Europe economist Jens Eisenschmidt, a former ECB official, says he
suspects the bank’s governing council “would prefer not to touch TPI”, highlighting the political
sensitivity of emergency bond buying. He also cites “scant evidence so far” that the rise in
borrowing costs is pushing the central bank’s monetary policy off course. “But surely, the ECB will
be closely monitoring that situation.”
Some insiders think it is just a matter of time before the ECB switches into crisis mode. There
could soon be change at the top. Lagarde’s mandate officially ends in October 2027 but she recently
said that she might be “leaving a few months before the end of my term”.
One former European central banker adds that the institution’s next leadership will have to walk a
fine line between preventing a crisis and escalating it: “There is a high probability that the next
president of the ECB at some point during their tenure will have to buy French government bonds.”
