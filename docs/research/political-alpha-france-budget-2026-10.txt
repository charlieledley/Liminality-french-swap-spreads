that Le Pen still leads in every single poll.
Emmanuel Macron is trying to lock in the EU’s next seven-year budget and the
succession at the European Central Bank (ECB) before he leaves office, but the RN’s
pledge to halve France’s net EU contribution makes any deal signed before May 2027
provisional.
What Has Changed Since September 14
Our September 14 note “France: Lecornu Will Survive, His Budget Will Not” said that the 2027
budget would be a placeholder, that the 4.9% deficit target floated in August would not survive
the return from vacation, and that the savings would come almost entirely from spending with
no general tax increase. All three held. The deficit target landed at 5.0%, the budget bill raises
no general taxes, and Lecornu is still in office. That said, the Socialists have moved from
possibly censuring the government over the budget to almost definitely doing so, and the bond
markets have added significant pressure over the past few weeks, forcing Lecornu to find
additional savings. Here is where things are right now:
The 2027 Budget. Lecornu presented his draft on October 1: €43 billion of new
measures, or €54 billion counting savings already in the pipeline, to cut the deficit from
5.4% of GDP this year to 5.0% in 2027, with no general tax increase. Pensions above
€1,260 a month will be indexed below inflation and civil service pay will be frozen. Even
so, debt rises from 119.3% to 121.7% of GDP, interest costs climb from €79 billion to
€91 billion, and the whole exercise assumes a rosy 1% growth next year, double this
year’s 0.5%.
France’s Referees. Scope Ratings downgraded France last week, and ECB President
Christine Lagarde, herself a former French finance minister, told La Croix on September
30 that debt approaching 120% of GDP with no clear path to control it is a serious
problem, adding that the pension system cannot carry on as it is. The gap between
French and German ten-year yields has since passed 140 basis points, the widest since
the height of the euro crisis in 2012. France now has the most expensive sovereign debt
of the entire Eurozone.

Digging Deeper – Why Le Pen Now Crucial to the Budget
Lecornu’s survival has always depended on one of the two large opposition blocs standing
aside. What has changed is which one.
The Socialists Have Walked Away. A Socialist abstention from censure now looks
unlikely. All four left-wing group leaders are now on the record that they would vote to
censure. Socialist Assembly leader Boris Vallaud said that he saw no “outstretched
hand” in Lecornu’s letter to his deputies, and on October 2 he, Senate leader Patrick
Kanner and deputy party leader Johanna Rolland wrote that the budget was “not
acceptable” as it stands. Their asks, a lettre rectificative (a formal rewrite of the bill by
the government itself) and more tax on high incomes, are ones Lecornu simply cannot
meet without losing the center-right.
Lecornu Has Changed the Arithmetic. On September 17 the PM promised to use
neither the 49.3 (the article that lets a government pass a bill without a vote, at the price
of a no-confidence motion) nor ordonnances (bringing the budget into force by decree
once parliament’s seventy days have run out), provided there is no “obstruction,” a
warning aimed at Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s far-left “France Unbowed” party. The escape
clause is wide, but an actual vote changes who matters. Abstentions do not count, so
the RN standing aside is enough to pass the budget against the entire left, while bringing
the government down still requires the RN and the whole left to vote together.
Marine Le Pen Wants a Budget She Can Rewrite. She told RN deputies in midSeptember that an “imperfect, operational” budget before the election was preferable to
a loi spéciale (the stopgap law that rolls over last year’s taxes), because the RN could
amend it once in power. But later she said she ruled nothing out, and her deputies say
they will judge the text on its merits. We read this as price-setting, but not as an empty
threat: in December 2024 she brought down Michel Barnier, with French spreads at what
were then their widest since 2012, after he had already met two of her demands and
refused a third on pension indexation. The difference now is that the election is six
months away and the budget is one she expects to inherit, and she does not need to
vote for it, only to abstain or stay off a censure motion. While the RN is leaning towards
non-censure, what makes it hard to predict is that Marine Le Pen tends to decide and
her party tends to follow. Lecornu insists there will be no negotiation with the RN, but

The Flashpoints Ahead
The next three weeks bring four events that will shape the budget fight before the decisive
votes, and none of them is likely to calm things down:
National Rally’s Counter-Budget (October 6). Le Pen will present the RN’s alternative
budget at a press conference tomorrow at 10am, with Jordan Bardella expected to
present it and Jean-Philippe Tanguy to handle the numbers. Pre-briefings point to €125
billion of savings over a five-year term, from closing state agencies, cutting immigrationrelated spending, fighting tax and social fraud, and unilaterally reducing France’s EU
contribution, alongside cuts to production taxes and a referendum on a constitutional
“golden rule” that would cap the deficit until debt is durably below 60% of GDP. Last
year’s counter-budget claimed €36 billion of savings for 2026, roughly a third of it from
immigration, and was widely judged not credible. The totals matter less than the red
lines. Tanguy has already called a pension freeze one, and Lecornu’s draft indexes
pensions above €1,260 a month below inflation, which the RN is likely to treat as a
freeze.
The Social Democratic Primary (October 9–10 and 16–17). Raphaël Glucksmann is
the favorite against Olivier Faure, Ségolène Royal, and two others, with Boris Vallaud in
his camp. The winner will set the Socialists’ budget line, and a “presidential” candidate
who wants to be taken seriously on the debt has little interest in owning a December
fiscal crisis.
The First Days in the Assembly (October 7 and 13). The bill goes to the Assembly's
finance committee on Wednesday and then on to the full chamber on October 13. The
first test of Lecornu's pledge comes on the floor, and it is whether Jean-Luc Mélenchon
and France Unbowed flood the chamber with amendments, which would give him the
"obstruction" he needs to reach for the 49.3, and give the RN and the Socialists a
pretext of their own.
Moody’s (October 23). Moody’s rates France Aa3 with a negative outlook, and its
scheduled review is expected to drop in the middle of the budget’s first reading. A
downgrade would hand Lecornu his best argument for passing it and add pressure on
RN to explain where they stand.

Le Pen takes 33–35% in the first round in every OpinionWay scenario tested in September, with
Mélenchon on 16–17%, and Harris Interactive’s September 28 runoff tests have her beating
Édouard Philippe 57–43 and Mélenchon 69–31. The scenario that has drawn attention since
our last note is a Le Pen–Mélenchon runoff, which becomes possible if Philippe and Gabriel
Attal keep splitting the center. We still see a Le Pen–centrist runoff as more likely, but the
center’s failure to consolidate is now the single largest variable in the presidential race.
The RN is trying to look serious but will not abandon pensioners. Jordan Bardella warned in
August that Mélenchon’s plan to cancel the debt held by the Banque de France could make
investors fear the same treatment for private creditors, and Le Pen’s slight preference for
passing Lecornu’s budget fits the same mold. Tomorrow’s counter-budget is the RN’s most
detailed attempt yet to explain how it would stabilize a debt ratio that, on the government’s own
numbers, only stops rising once the deficit is back to 3%, a target now pushed to 2029.
Mélenchon keeps cancellation at the center of his campaign, and Lagarde again called it
financially very dangerous last week. In September we flagged the risk of a softer RN version
by spring. So far the RN has moved the other way, and we would now treat a Le Pen embrace
of the idea as less likely.
Europe Is Racing Against the Clock
The French election is also running into Europe’s own calendar. Macron’s instinct is to settle as
much as he can before May, while the RN’s is to make anything settled before May look
illegitimate.
The EU Budget. The Irish Council presidency will put its first figures to EU ministers on
October 13, ahead of the leaders’ summit on October 15, with the aim of a deal by yearend. Paris says that without substantial new “own resources” (revenue raised at EU
level) deadlock is guaranteed, while Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden want a
smaller budget focused on new priorities. Bardella has pledged to halve France’s €12–
15 billion net contribution — a cut the counter-budget is expected to make unilaterally
— and accuses Brussels of trying to lock the budget in before a change of majority.
Right now, we do not expect a deal before the French election, and any deal Macron
signs would face a French government with every incentive to reopen it and unanimity
rules that make that painful for everyone else.
The ECB. Reuters reported on September 15 that Paris would back Klaas Knot, the
former Dutch central bank governor, to succeed Lagarde if a French candidate became
chief economist, and Lagarde has said she could leave a few months before her term
ends in October 2027. An early handover means Macron, not his successor, shapes the
ECB leadership that would face any French fiscal crisis. We expect the RN to make this

The Fiscal Rules. France has been under the EU’s excessive deficit procedure since
July 2024, and the medium-term plan it agreed with Brussels put the 2027 deficit at
4.1%, against 5.0% in the draft. We expect the European Commission to note the gap in
its November opinion and do little else before May, since nobody in their right mind in
Brussels wants to hand Le Pen a campaign against EU austerity.
How This Could All Play Out
We see three ways the autumn ends. The first is slightly more likely than the other two, but the
gap has narrowed enough that clients should not plan around it.
1. The RN Stands Aside. Le Pen extracts a visible concession, most likely on pensions,
and abstains or stays off the censure motions. The budget passes in December, but only
after weeks in which every RN statement is read as a signal, and with a text the next
president has already promised to rewrite. Tomorrow’s counter-budget is the first place
to look for what that concession, if any, will be.
2. An RN Hardening. A Le Pen statement tying the RN’s votes to a demand Lecornu
cannot meet would bring the government down with the left’s votes, as could Lecornu
invoking “obstruction” to reach for the 49.3 before the RN has anything to show for its
restraint. Lecornu would then face ordonnances, which no government has used since
1958, or a third loi spéciale, and Macron the choice between yet another prime minister
and another dissolution.
3. A Crisis That Removes the Deal. A sharp deterioration in France’s borrowing
conditions cuts both ways. It raises the cost to Le Pen of being the one who brings the
government down, but it also leaves Lecornu unable to pay her price, since reversing
the pension savings would read as abandoning the budget at the worst moment. A crisis
in which he cannot concede on pensions is the scenario in which we would expect
censure, and the one clients should be most prepared for.
